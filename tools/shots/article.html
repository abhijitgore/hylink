The quiet parts of a browser

Interfaces · 8 min read

Most of what a browser does for you, it does without asking. Pages are prefetched before you decide to visit them, fonts are swapped in mid-render, and a tab you have not touched in an hour is quietly frozen to give the rest of the machine some room. None of it announces itself. That is the point.

The interesting design question is not what a browser can do, but what it is willing to interrupt you for. Every notification, every prompt, every little badge is a claim on attention that has to be earned. The best interfaces spend that budget carefully, and the ones that wear you down are usually the ones that spend it on everything.

Hovering is where this gets subtle. A pointer resting somewhere is a weak signal — it might mean interest, or it might mean a hand left on a mouse while someone reads. Treating those two the same is how you end up with tooltips flickering across a page like weather. The trick is to wait for the pointer to actually settle before responding at all.

There is a version of this argument about links specifically. A link is a decision point, and the browser gives you exactly one gesture for it by default: click, and go. Everything else — a new tab, a new window, a private window — lives behind a right-click and a menu you have to read. It works, but it costs a beat every time.

None of which is a complaint, exactly. It is more that the defaults were set a long time ago, when there were fewer tabs and fewer ways to open them, and nobody has had much reason to revisit them since.
